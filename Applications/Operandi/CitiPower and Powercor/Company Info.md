# CitiPower and Powercor

Operate as electricity distribution network service providers (DNSPs) under the parent entity Victoria Power Networks (VPN). They manage critical energy infrastructure, delivering electricity to over 1.2 million homes and businesses across Victoria.

## Parent Company

### VPN: Victoria Power Networks
- 51% Owned by CK Infrastructure Holdings (CKI) and Power Assets Holdings (PAH), which are members of the Cheung Kong Group listed in Hong Kong

- 49% Owned by Spark Infrastructure, which is managed by KKR, the Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan Board, and the Public Sector Pension Investment Board.

## Major Services
### Electricity Distribution & Network Management
Taking power from high-voltage transmission interconnectors and stepping it down to safely deliver it to end consumers.  
### Asset Maintenance & Outage Response
24/7 emergency response, fault repair, vegetation management, and ongoing maintenance of poles, transformers, and sub-transmission lines.  
### Energy Transition & DER Integration
Orchestrating Distributed Energy Resources (DER), including two-way rooftop solar export management, dynamic connections, community batteries, and electric vehicle (EV) smart charging infrastructure.  
### Metering & Connection Services
Meter deployment, smart meter data collection, and connection management for residential builds through to large commercial developments.
### Bushfire Mitigation & Network Safety
Leading high-voltage safety engineering, including the rollout of Rapid Earth Fault Current Limiter (REFCL) technology across high-risk rural networks.  
### Commercial Services (Beon Energy Solutions)
A commercial arm building renewable energy farms, large-scale battery storage installations, and bespoke grid connections.

## Business Operation
Powercor primarily operates within the **electricity distribution** segment of the National Electricity Market (NEM), but its activities extend across several regulated, operational, and commercial domains:

---

### 1. Regulated Electricity Distribution (Core Business)

As a Distribution Network Service Provider (DNSP), Powercor operates as an economically regulated natural monopoly under the Australian Energy Regulator (AER):

* **Standard Control Services (Network Distribution):** Transporting electricity from transmission terminal stations (via 66 kV, 22 kV, and low-voltage lines) to more than 900,000 homes, farms, and businesses across central and western Victoria.
* **Network Reliability & Bushfire Mitigation:** Managing physical poles and wires, tree clearance, asset replacement, and specialized safety systems (such as REFCL technology in high-fire-risk rural areas).
* **Alternative Control Services (Direct Customer Services):** Fee-for-service regulated activities requested by individual customers or retailers, such as new premises connections, service line alterations, and temporary disconnections/reconnections.

---

### 2. Metering & Market Data Services

Unlike many states where metering was fully contestable from the start, Victoria mandated distributor-led smart metering:

* **Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI):** Deploying, maintaining, and operating digital smart meters for all mass-market customers on its network.
* **Market Settlement & Data Provision:** Collecting 5-minute interval consumption data and feeding it through AEMO's market systems (MSATS/e-Hub) to energy retailers for consumer billing and wholesale financial settlement.

---

### 3. Distribution System Operator (DSO) & DER Orchestration

As rooftop solar, home batteries, and electric vehicles proliferate, Powercor acts as an active distribution system orchestrator:

* **Two-Way Energy Facilitation:** Managing export capacity for hundreds of thousands of residential and commercial rooftop solar installations.
* **Dynamic Operating Envelopes (DOEs):** Calculating real-time variable import/export limits for smart inverters to prevent feeder voltage breaches and reverse-power congestion.
* **Community & Neighbourhood Batteries:** Integrating community-scale storage into distribution substations to soak up daytime solar exports and support peak evening local demand.

---

### 4. Commercial & Contestable Works (Beon Energy Solutions)

Under AER Ring-Fencing Guidelines, DNSPs cannot use regulated distribution revenue to subsidize competitive market activities. Victoria Power Networks operates Beon Energy Solutions as a separate commercial arm to compete in unregulated areas:

* **Renewable Infrastructure Construction:** Engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) for large-scale solar farms, wind farms, and grid-scale battery storage (BESS).
* **Major Grid Connections:** Designing and building dedicated substations and high-voltage transmission/distribution connections for major commercial developments, data centers, and renewable energy generators.

---

### Areas Powercor Does Not Operate

* **Energy Retail:** Powercor does not sell electricity, formulate retail plans, or bill consumers for energy consumption (bills are issued by retailers like AGL, Origin, or Amber).
* **Wholesale Generation Ownership:** Powercor does not own or trade wholesale merchant generators in the NEM spot market.
* **Bulk High-Voltage Transmission:** Powercor operates the distribution grid up to 66 kV; long-distance 220 kV / 500 kV transmission lines in Victoria are owned by AusNet Services and directed by AEMO.

## CAPEX Budgeting Cycle & Government Regulation
- CitiPower and Powercor are economic monopolies regulated by the Australian Energy Regulator (AER) under the National Electricity Law (NEL) and National Electricity Rules (NER).  

- The 5-Year Regulatory Determination ("Reset"):Capital and operational budgets are governed on a five-year reset cycle. The current regulatory period runs from 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2031 (following the AER's 2026 Final Determination).  

- Around 18–24 months before each cycle, the businesses submit a detailed, costed regulatory proposal outlining projected capital expenditure (Capex) and operational expenditure (Opex).

- The "Building Block" Framework: AER determines allowable revenue using a building block approach: Capex additions grow the RAB, earning a return on capital (calculated via the Weighted Average Cost of Capital, or WACC), along with regulatory depreciation.Capital 

- Expenditure Criteria & Justification
    - Capex proposals must demonstrate prudency and efficiency under NER Rule 6.5.7. 
        - Expenditures are grouped into replacement (repex), system growth (augex), DER integration, and IT/security.
    - Any major discrete network augmentation above AER-mandated thresholds must undergo a Regulatory Investment Test for Distribution (RIT-D) to prove it represents the highest net economic benefit compared to non-network alternatives (e.g., demand management, local batteries).
- Capital Expenditure Sharing Scheme (CESS):During the 5-year delivery window, internal Capex budgeting is driven by the CESS incentive mechanism. If the business delivers approved projects efficiently below the AER Capex allowance, it retains a share of the savings; if it overspends, shareholders bear a portion of the penalty.

## How CitiPower and Powercor Relate to AER 
- CitiPower & Powercor $\leftrightarrow$ AER (The Economic Arbitrator)Revenue Caps & Allowed Return: CitiPower and Powercor cannot set their own tariffs. Every five years, the AER reviews their proposals and determines their total allowable revenue based on the Regulated Asset Base (RAB), benchmarked efficient operational costs, and the allowed Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC).

- Expenditure Approvals: Every dollar of major capital spend (e.g., pole replacements, feeder upgrades, digital network management) must satisfy AER prudency tests and pass the Regulatory Investment Test for Distribution (RIT-D).

- Performance Incentives: The AER manages schemes that directly affect distributor profitability:
    - STPIS (Service Target Performance Incentive Scheme): Penalties or bonuses based on unplanned outage duration (SAIDI) and frequency 
    - (SAIFI).CESS & EBSS: Incentives for capital and operational efficiency.

## How CitiPower and Powercor Relate to AEMO
- CitiPower & Powercor $\leftrightarrow$ AEMO (The Operational Partner) Real-Time Transmission Interface: While CitiPower and Powercor operate distribution assets (up to 66 kV), AEMO manages the bulk high-voltage transmission grid (220 kV / 500 kV) in Victoria. Powercor and CitiPower must balance feeder loads and coordinate switching at terminal stations directly with AEMO.

- DER Visibility & Minimum Operational Demand: High rooftop solar penetration in Powercor’s territory frequently pushes net daytime demand to record lows. AEMO and Powercor collaborate closely on emergency backstop mechanisms (solar curtailment protocols) and inverter standards (AS/NZS 4777.2) to prevent grid instability during negative-demand events.

- Connections & System Security: For large-scale distributed generation (e.g., solar farms, community batteries connecting to the 22 kV or 66 kV distribution lines), Powercor must liaise with AEMO regarding system strength, fault levels, and power quality modeling.

## The AER–AEMO Relationship
While legally independent, the AER and AEMO coordinate closely:

- Forecasting and Planning Alignment: AEMO publishes the biennial Integrated System Plan (ISP) and Electricity Statement of Opportunities (ESOO) forecasting grid requirements. The AER uses AEMO’s demand forecasts and ISP transmission needs as independent benchmarks when evaluating network businesses' Capex augmentation requests.

- System Incident Investigation: When significant grid events or blackouts occur (such as extreme storms knocking down transmission towers or triggering widespread distribution tripping), AEMO publishes the technical operational review, while the AER reviews market compliance and participant bidding behavior.

- Transition Governance: AEMO designs the future operational architecture for distributed energy and consumer data sharing (such as Project EDGE and the National Distributed Energy Resources Register), while the AER regulates how DNSPs are compensated for the software, smart metering, and infrastructure needed to deliver those capabilities.

## 1. Real-Time Grid Operations & Telemetry (AEMO Interface)
### Managing the physical grid and coordinating with AEMO requires low-latency, mission-critical systems
    - ADMS (Advanced Distribution Management System):Unifies SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition), OMS (Outage Management System), and advanced distribution power-flow applications.  
    - Powers FLISR (Fault Location, Isolation, and Service Restoration), automatically switching circuit breakers to restore power around faulted lines within seconds. 

### HV-DERMS & LV Dynamic Envelopes
    - Powercor uses a specialized High Voltage DERMS to dynamically throttle or ramp large commercial solar/wind farms in real time rather than refusing connection capacity.  
    - Integrates Dynamic Operating Envelopes (DOEs) down to the residential low-voltage network, publishing dynamically calculated import/export limits every 5 to 15 minutes to solar inverters and EV chargers.

### Inter-Control Center Communications Protocol (ICCP / IEC 60870-6)
- The primary telemetry data highway linking CitiPower and Powercor’s control rooms directly with AEMO's National Electricity Market dispatch systems.
- Streams real-time line voltages, feeder loads, switch positions, and zone substation data so AEMO can run state estimators and evaluate transmission-distribution boundary stability.

## 2. Market Systems & Data Interchange (AEMO B2B / B2M)
Because Victoria implemented smart meters across nearly 100% of residential and commercial premises, data throughput is massive:

### AEMO e-Hub & aseXML Messaging
- The standardized transaction gateway between DNSPs, electricity retailers, and AEMO.
- Relies on aseXML (Australian Standard for Energy XML) payloads to automate retail workflows: customer transfers, move-ins/move-outs, meter service orders, and field-fault service notifications.

### MSATS (Market Settlement and Transfer Solution):
- AEMO’s core market registry. CitiPower and Powercor push and pull National Metering Identifier (NMI) standing data, connection statuses, and loss-factor codes to ensure retailers and generators settle wholesale financial markets accurately.

### MDMS (Meter Data Management System):
- Handles five-minute interval metering data collected from millions of smart meters via advanced mesh and cellular radio networks.
- Cleanses, validates, and estimates (VEE process) interval load consumption before pushing bulk interval meter data into AEMO's settlement systems.

## 3. Enterprise Asset Management & AER Regulatory Reporting
Data submitted to the AER must be audit-grade, linking field work and capital asset additions to the Regulated Asset Base (RAB):

### Enterprise Asset Management & ERP (e.g., SAP)
- Tracks the entire lifecycle of physical plant—poles, wires, transformers, and switchgear—from Capex project commissioning to routine maintenance work orders and eventual write-offs.
- Segregates Capex and Opex accurately according to regulatory accounting guidelines, maintaining the ledger for the Capital Expenditure Sharing Scheme (CESS).

### Regulatory Information Notice (RIN) Reporting Engines
- The AER audits DNSPs through annual Category Analysis and Economic Benchmarking RINs.
- Dedicated business intelligence pipelines pull from GIS, financial ledgers, and SCADA historians to quantify metrics such as asset unit costs, failure rates, total route length, and transformer capacity utilisation.

### STPIS Outage Analytics (OMS Data Warehousing)
- Calculates SAIDI (System Average Interruption Duration Index) and SAIFI (System Average Interruption Frequency Index) down to the millisecond.
- Because small fractions of an hour determine multi-million dollar penalties or bonuses under the AER’s Service Target Performance Incentive Scheme (STPIS), outage logs require automated cross-referencing between OMS switching logs and weather-exclusion event records.

### A. Planned Work (Asset Maintenance, Inspections, Capital Projects)
- Lifecycle: Generated inside the central Enterprise Asset Management (EAM) system based on maintenance cycles (e.g., periodic pole testing, feeder thermal imaging, crossarm inspection) or capital program schedules.

- Planning & Dispatch: Works are bundled geographically to minimize travel time and matched against crew competencies (e.g., live-line work, elevated work platform certifications, sub-transmission qualifications).

- Execution: Field crews pull detailed asset specifications, single-line diagrams, and bill-of-materials from their mobile devices, log condition test results, record asset degradation photos, and push completion records back to the enterprise ledger.

### B. Unplanned Work (Emergency Restoration & Fault Dispatch)
- Trigger: Initiated either automatically via SCADA tripping signals, smart meter "last gasp" notifications, or customer call-center reports.

- Priority Routing: Routed through the Distribution Control Centre (DCC) and the Outage Management System (OMS). Priority is determined by public safety (e.g., lines down, vegetation fires) and regulatory STPIS targets (minimizing customer interruption minutes).

- Coordination with Control Room: For any high-voltage switching, the field crew liaises directly with network operating controllers using electronic or radio switching sheets, establishing safe work tags, physical earths, and Permits to Work (PTW) before touching lines.

# Australian Electricity Supply Chain Actors

---

### 1. Generation (Competitive Wholesale Market)
Generators convert primary energy sources into physical electricity and sell it into the wholesale spot market.

* **Primary Assets:** Utility-scale solar farms, wind farms, grid-scale batteries (BESS), hydroelectric plants, and traditional thermal plants (coal and gas).
* **Role:** Submit offers to produce specific volumes of megawatts ($MW$) across dispatch intervals.
* **Commercial Model:** Earn revenue by dispatching energy into the wholesale spot market and entering financial hedging contracts (e.g., Power Purchase Agreements, caps, and swaps) to secure fixed-price cash flows against spot price volatility.
* **Key Australian Examples:** AGL, Origin Energy, EnergyAustralia, Neoen, Tilt Renewables, Hydro Tasmania.

---

### 2. Transmission Network Service Providers (TNSPs – Regulated Monopolies)
Transmission networks act as the high-voltage "freeway system," bulk-transporting power at high voltages (typically 220 kV to 500 kV) over long distances to minimize energy losses.

* **Primary Assets:** Tall steel lattice towers, extra-high-voltage lines, subsea cables, terminal stations, and interconnector links between state borders.
* **Role:** Transport electricity from remote Renewable Energy Zones (REZs) and large generation hubs directly to urban terminal stations and heavy industrial users (such as aluminum smelters).
* **Commercial Model:** Natural monopolies subject to economic regulation. They do not buy or sell energy; they recover revenue through AER-regulated asset charges (TUOS: Transmission Use of System).
* **Key Australian Examples:** AusNet Services (Victoria), Transgrid (NSW/ACT), Powerlink (Queensland), ElectraNet (South Australia), TasNetworks (Tasmania).

---

### 3. Distribution Network Service Providers (DNSPs – Regulated Monopolies)
Distribution networks step down electricity at terminal substations (to 66 kV, 22 kV, down to 400 V / 230 V) to distribute power locally across neighborhoods, commercial centers, and homes.

* **Primary Assets:** Zone substations, distribution transformers, power poles, underground feeder cables, switchgear, and customer meters.
* **Role:** Ensure local grid reliability, manage voltage stability, maintain physical assets, and facilitate two-way energy flows as rooftop solar and electric vehicles scale.
* **Commercial Model:** Natural monopolies regulated under revenue-cap determinations by the economic regulator. Network charges (DUOS: Distribution Use of System) make up a significant portion of retail bills.
* **Key Australian Examples:** CitiPower and Powercor, Jemena, United Energy, Ausgrid, Endeavour Energy, Energex, SA Power Networks.

---

### 4. Retailers (Competitive Consumer Market)
Retailers serve as the customer-facing commercial interface, purchasing wholesale electricity and packaging it into consumer plans.

* **Role:** Buy bulk wholesale electricity (exposed to spot and contract markets) and repackage it into bundled tariffs for households and commercial customers.
* **Responsibilities:**
  * Customer service, metering data management, and monthly billing.
  * Collecting total network fees (DUOS + TUOS) on behalf of distributors and transmission operators.
  * Paying customers for rooftop solar exports via Feed-in Tariffs (FiT).
  * Hedging financial exposure between fixed consumer rates and volatile wholesale market clearing prices.
* **Key Australian Examples:** Origin Energy, AGL, EnergyAustralia, Amber Electric, Red Energy, OVO Energy.

---

### 5. Emerging Actors: Distributed Energy & Aggregators
The line between consumers, distributors, and generators has blurred due to decentralization:

* **Prosumers:** Households and commercial buildings generating their own power via rooftop solar, storing it in residential batteries, and charging electric vehicles.
* **Virtual Power Plant (VPP) Aggregators:** Software and market platforms that aggregate thousands of small consumer batteries and smart inverters into a single dispatchable block, bidding aggregate capacity into the wholesale energy and frequency control (FCAS) markets.
* **Metering Coordinators & Data Providers:** Specialized entities deploying, maintaining, and reading advanced digital meters (smart meters) that stream interval consumption data to market operators and network planners.

### Companies That Are Alike (Peers / DNSPs)

These companies share the exact same business model, regulatory regime (AER 5-year resets, STPIS, CESS/EBSS), and operational responsibilities as CitiPower and Powercor. They do not compete with one another; each operates a geographical natural monopoly over a defined network footprint:

* **United Energy:** Operates the distribution network across Melbourne’s south-eastern suburbs and the Mornington Peninsula. Like CitiPower and Powercor, it manages poles, wires, substations, smart meters, and local outage restoration.
* **Jemena (Electricity Distribution):** Operates the distribution grid across Melbourne’s north-western suburbs. It faces similar urban and growth-corridor network challenges, operating under the same distribution pricing rules.
* **AusNet Services (Distribution Arm):** Operates the electricity distribution network across eastern and north-eastern Victoria. Geographically and operationally, AusNet’s rural distribution footprint strongly resembles Powercor's—both handle vast rural terrain, long radial feeders, and high bushfire-risk mitigation (including REFCL deployment).

---

### Companies That Are Different

These companies operate in different market tiers under distinct commercial models, incentives, and operational rules:

* **AusNet Services (Transmission Arm) — High-Voltage Transmission (TNSP):**
  * *Why it's different:* While AusNet runs a regional distribution network, it also owns and maintains Victoria's statewide high-voltage transmission backbone (220 kV to 500 kV towers and lines). It moves bulk electricity from remote power stations to terminal stations, coordinating directly with AEMO rather than distributing power to household street-level meters.
* **Electricity Retailers (AGL, Origin Energy, EnergyAustralia, Red Energy, Amber):**
  * *Why they're different:* Retailers operate in a fully competitive market. They do not own the physical grid. Instead, they buy wholesale electricity, hedge price volatility, manage customer accounts, issue billing, and package retail tariffs. If a customer changes retailer, the physical wires owned by Powercor or CitiPower do not change.
* **Wholesale Generators (Neoen, Tilt Renewables, AGL Generation, EnergyAustralia):**
  * *Why they're different:* These businesses own generation assets (such as wind farms, utility-scale solar farms, battery storage facilities, and gas/coal plants). They make money by producing and bidding electricity into AEMO’s 5-minute wholesale dispatch market, exposing themselves to commodity pricing rather than AER-regulated revenue caps.
* **Energy Aggregators / Virtual Power Plants (VPPs):**
  * *Why they're different:* Software-driven market participants that aggregate residential solar and home battery systems to trade capacity in wholesale energy or frequency control (FCAS) markets, coordinating between retailers, consumers, and AEMO rather than maintaining physical network lines.
